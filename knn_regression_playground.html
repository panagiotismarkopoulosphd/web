Theory

k-Nearest Neighbors regression and the bias–variance trade-off.

Data-generating process

Observations follow

$$ y = f(x) + e $$

where \( f \) is a fixed polynomial of degree \( d_f \) (unknown to the learner), and the noise \( e \) satisfies \( \mathbb{E}[e] = 0 \) and is independent of \( x \). Noise variance is \( \sigma^2 \). Inputs \( x \) are drawn uniformly from \( [-1, 1] \).

The coefficients of the true polynomial are fixed once \( d_f \) is selected.

The model: \( k \)-Nearest Neighbors

k-NN regression is a non-parametric method. For a query point \( x \), the prediction is the average of the responses of the \( K \) training points closest to \( x \) (measured by absolute distance \( |x_i - x| \)):

$$ \hat{f}(x) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_K(x)} y_i $$

where \( \mathcal{N}_K(x) \) is the set of the \( K \) nearest neighbors of \( x \) in the training set. There is no parametric form and no coefficient vector to estimate; the prediction is determined solely by the training data and the choice of \( K \).

Small \( K \) produces a flexible, high-variance fit (the prediction can change sharply with small changes in the training set). Large \( K \) produces a smoother, higher-bias fit (the prediction averages over a wider neighborhood and cannot track rapid changes in \( f \)).

What the axes mean

Main plot

  • Horizontal axis: input feature \( x \in [-1, 1] \).
  • Vertical axis: response \( y \).

Blue markers: one training set of size \( N \). Black dashed curve: true polynomial \( f \). Orange curve: the \( K \)-NN predictor evaluated on a dense grid. Optional green band: mean prediction ± one standard deviation across independent training sets.

Bottom plot

  • Horizontal axis: number of neighbors \( K \).
  • Vertical axis: bias², variance, and bias² + variance + \( \sigma^2 \), each averaged over \( x \).

Bias–variance decomposition

For a fixed \( x \),

$$ \mathbb{E}\bigl[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\bigr] = \bigl(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x)\bigr)^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}(x)) + \sigma^2 $$

The expectation and variance are over the random training set.

  • Bias² tends to increase with \( K \) because larger neighborhoods average away local structure of \( f \).
  • Variance tends to decrease with \( K \) because the average of more neighbors is more stable across training sets.
  • \( \sigma^2 \) is irreducible.

An intermediate \( K \) typically minimizes expected test error.

Monte Carlo estimation

Bias and variance are estimated by drawing \( R \) independent training sets, computing the \( K \)-NN predictor on each, evaluating the predictors on a dense grid, and computing the sample mean and variance of the predictions at each grid point. Bias² is the squared difference between the mean prediction and true \( f(x) \). Results are averaged over the grid.

Train MSE versus test MSE

  • Train MSE is the average squared residual on the training points. For \( K = 1 \) it is typically near zero (each point is its own nearest neighbor). It rises as \( K \) increases.
  • Test MSE is computed on a large independent test set and reflects generalization. It usually shows a U-shape as a function of \( K \).
